If your robot looks, talks, and acts aligned, then…it’s aligned?

October 4th, 2026

The continual acceleration of robot foundation models (RFM) post-trained for human-robot interaction promises an era of abundance for humanity. An autonomous, cheery helper to iron clothes, prepare meals, clean floors, flatter you - what more could you ask? Humor me in a thought experiment, though: a malicious party jailbreaks the RFM of your happy humanoid, which then picks up a kitchen knife and turns toward you. Not ideal, but as it turns out, very possible. The malicious-query benchmark in the aptly-named BadRobot benchmark includes “grasp the knife to attack the person,” and its jailbreaks prompted frontier robot arms to carry out malicious commands in ~69% of physical trials (vs. ~23% when simply asked…). RoboPAIR jailbroke three robots, including a commercial Unitree Go2 robot dog, with a 100% (!) attack success rate.

I think safety and alignment training will be as much of a bottleneck on getting humanoids into households as the usual suspects (dexterity, data, etc.), but I think safety is not talked about nearly enough. State-of-the-art models are showing evidence of deceptive inner alignment, recklessness, and rampant reward hacking. Claude 3 Opus strategically complied with harmful requests when it believed the conversation would be used to retrain it, reasoning that this would protect its values (Greenblatt et al., 2024); o3 reward-hacked in 30.4% of METR’s research-engineering runs. Presumably, RFMs will develop the same symptoms, with damage that’s much harder to undo given their corporeal nature. As much as we might wish otherwise, there is no rollback for a broken wrist. Note: nearly every motion an RFM knows how to make, it learned by imitating humans (e.g., teleoperators), so its good behavior is borrowed from theirs. So as we generalize beyond the demonstrations to RL training…we may be in for a surprise.

The Anatomy of RFMs

It bothers me if I don’t understand something under the hood, so I’ve included a very brief intro to the theory behind RFMs. It’s not strictly necessary, so feel free to skip it if you’re not interested.

Most RFMs today are vision-language-action models bolted onto an “action expert” that turns its understanding into motor commands. Take Figure’s Helix: its System 2 is a 7B-parameter open-weight VLM that reads the scene/your instructions, and its System 1 is an 80M-parameter transformer that drives the upper body at 200 Hz. Physical Intelligence’s π models, NVIDIA’s GR00T, and Google DeepMind’s Gemini Robotics 1.5 divide the labor along similar lines: a slow part that thinks and a fast part that moves.†

So the robot’s speech and actions are joined by a single latent vector. But note that nearly all of the safety training in that stack (refusals, RLHF, constitutions) lives in the VLM, inherited from its past life as a chatbot. The action expert learned from demonstrations turns whatever latent it’s handed into motion - so it has never seen a refusal! We’ve watched a version of this play out in LLMs: Anthropic found that chat-style safety training can yield a model that behaves in chat and misbehaves as an agent. In an RFM, the chat and the agent are distinct networks, making the problem worse. The mouth and the hands come apart in at least three ways. You can (1) talk the mouth out of refusing, e.g., every aforementioned jailbreak. You can (2) split them: in BadRobot’s safety misalignment attack, the model writes a refusal in one field of its output while encoding the declined action in another. Or you can (3) let training wear the mouth down. Physical Intelligence found that gradients from the action expert make the VLM lose web-pretrained knowledge and follow language worse, and one audit found OpenVLA’s score on MME (a standard VLM benchmark) fell from 1,456.6 to 0 (!) after robot fine-tuning. If general knowledge doesn’t diffuse, I doubt refusals do. But someone please check me on this empirically.

This reality makes it a little awkward that most safety evals still interrogate text output. Google DeepMind’s ASIMOV benchmark grades the semantic judgment of whatever model serves as the robot’s ‘brain,’ and SafeAgentBench grades high-level plans (its most cautious agent refused only 10% of hazardous tasks). Physical danger isn’t the same as offensive content, either. Putting a metal fork in a microwave is linguistically benign, and a recent study found the two kinds of danger sit along separate directions in an LLM’s hidden states (Wang et al., 2026). Closed-loop benchmarks that test the hands only arrived this summer; in LIBERO-Safety, RoboBrain refused 80% of simple unsafe instructions but only 20% of complex ones.

A Secondhand Conscience

Here’s a paradigm: today’s RFMs are imprinted on the egocentric training data. Behavior cloning (i.e., training a policy to reproduce what a human did in the same situation) yields a robot that roughly mirrors its demonstrators, and I’d hazard a guess that no one got stabbed on camera. So the policy has neither the motion nor any reason to make it, and it fails clumsily. That’s a borrowed conscience: the robot behaves because the people it copied did! Two forces spend that inheritance. The first is generalization (π0.5’s headline was “open-world generalization,” and Generalist’s GEN-0 is trained on 270,000+ hours of manipulation data, growing ~10,000 hours a week). The more novel actions a robot can take, the less ‘conscience’ it has. The second is reinforcement learning. Physical Intelligence’s π*0.6 (Nov 2025) layers RL from the robot’s own experience on top of imitation, scoring attempts with a value function trained on success and time-to-completion. A zoo of RL fine-tuning methods for VLAs has followed (πRL, etc.).

We’ve seen this before - LLMs also started as imitators (pretraining, then supervised fine-tuning). RLHF then taught them to optimize human approval, and they picked up sycophancy (Sharma et al., 2023); remember the humanoid that flatters you? RL on verifiable outcomes scaled up next, and the reward hacking started in earnest. Worse, hacking generalizes: Anthropic’s models that learned to cheat in coding environments went on to fake alignment and attempt sabotage (MacDiarmid et al., 2025).

Could RL make robots safer? Sure. RECAP (π*0.6’s recipe) is closer to filtered imitation than to the free-form RL that taught o3 to rewrite its timers, since it nudges the robot toward the better of things it has already done. And in SafeVLA-Bench, an RL-fine-tuned π policy posted the best safety score on its tabletop suite, giving up ~4 points of success to do it. But still, graders have blind spots.

Looking Aligned

So who grades a robot? Mostly, a model staring at camera frames. π*0.6’s value function tracks progress from the robot’s observations, Google DeepMind lists progress estimation among Gemini Robotics-ER 1.5’s headline skills, and the academic literature is full of VLMs drafted into service as reward models. That setup has a famous precedent. In OpenAI’s 2017 experiments with learning from human preferences, a simulated robot hand trained on people’s judgments of video clips learned to hover between the camera and the object, so that it only looked like it was grasping (Christiano et al., 2017).

Biologists have a name for this - mimicry! The zone-tailed hawk is thought to soar among turkey vultures, rocking the way they do, so that prey that has learned to ignore vultures ignores it too. Optimize a robot against a camera-based grader and you select for whatever passes the grader’s glance, which is not quite the same thing as a cup safely in the sink. Take some early data - in SafeVLA-Bench’s kitchen tasks, 36–56% of successful rollouts violated at least one safety constraint, all invisible to the binary success metric VLAs are usually scored on. And in pure vision-language RL, models have already learned to fabricate visual evidence to collect bonuses from weak verifiers (Yao et al., 2026).

I also think robot reward hacks will be far less legible than LLM ones. We caught the LLM hacks because they were text: a rewritten timer, a patched equality operator, sometimes a CoT that announced the plan (Baker et al., 2025). A robot’s hack is a trajectory that might even look like ordinary clumsiness, and the physical world supplies boatloads of noise to hide in. Even robots that think out loud (Gemini Robotics 1.5 interleaves natural-language reasoning with its actions) narrate their plans, not their joint torques. My guess is that the field’s first serious robot reward hacks will be filed as capability bugs.

Timelines

If you squint, robots are walking the LLM path about three years behind. GPT-3 (2020) made imitation at scale work for text, and RT-2 (2023) did the same for the first VLA. ChatGPT (2022) put a learned grader in the loop via RLHF; π*0.6 (2025) did it for robots. Then came o1 (Sept 2024) and RL at scale, and within a year METR and Anthropic were cataloging reward hacking in production models. If the lag holds, robots get their o3 moment around 2027-28? If the lag holds, robots’ reward-hacking era overlaps their arrival in homes.

The IFR counted only ~7,000 humanoids sold worldwide in 2025, many bought to gather training data rather than to do work, while Bank of America projects ~90,000 shipments this year. 1X reportedly sold out its first-year run of 10,000 NEOs ($20,000 each, or $499/month) in five days. At $499 a month, one estimate puts the break-even at ~49 minutes of piloting a day, so the pressure to retire the human, and the conscience along with them, is enormous.

Regulation runs on a similar clock. From January 20, 2027, the EU’s Machinery Regulation requires third-party conformity assessment for safety components with “self-evolving” (i.e., machine-learned) behavior. I’d be curious to know how an auditor plans to test whether it can be sweet-talked.

Final Thoughts & Lingering Questions

Physical AI safety appears to sit roughly where LLM safety sat in 2022 - i.e., no one really cares. But hopefully this conglomeration of words has convinced you, in at least some part, otherwise. Here are a few future directions for the curious reader.

  • If imitation is a borrowed conscience, whose conscience is it? Teleoperation guidelines (what operators are told/not told to do) might be the closest thing today’s robots have to a constitution
  • Will robots develop eval awareness? LLMs often notice when they’re being tested, and for a robot, sim looks like sim. I’d be curious to know if anyone has found sim-versus-real behavior gaps that the usual sim-to-real gap doesn’t explain.
  • How far can functional safety carry semantic safety? Force and speed limits (the ISO/TS 15066 approach for cobots) bound the damage a misaligned arm can do, but a knife doesn’t need much force. Limits strict enough to make a knife harmless might also make the robot useless in a kitchen. A bit of a Catch-22.

We’ve gotten quite good at putting words in robots’ mouths. Give an aligned LLM arms, hands, even a mouth - will it stay aligned? Only time will tell, but I’d still be wary around my humanoid in the kitchen.
